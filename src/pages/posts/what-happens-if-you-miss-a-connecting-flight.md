# What Happens If You Miss a Connecting Flight?

The answer to this question depends almost entirely on one thing: **whether your connecting flights were booked as a single itinerary, or as two separate tickets.** This one detail determines whether missing a connection is a manageable inconvenience the airline sorts out for you, or an expensive problem you have to solve yourself, on the spot, at the airport.

This guide covers what actually happens in each scenario, the steps to take immediately if it happens to you, and how to reduce the risk in the first place.

---

## The Short Answer

**If your flights are on one linked itinerary (protected connection):** The airline is responsible for getting you to your final destination. They'll typically rebook you on the next available flight at no extra cost, and in many cases provide meals or accommodation if the delay is significant or overnight.

**If your flights are two separate bookings (self-transfer):** The second airline has no obligation to you at all. Your original ticket for the missed flight is simply gone, and you'll generally need to buy a new one at whatever the current fare happens to be.

If you don't already know which category your booking falls into, that's the first thing to check — ideally before you travel, but immediately if you're already dealing with a missed connection.

---

## What Happens With a Protected Connection

If your itinerary was booked as a single ticket — whether on one airline or across alliance partners with an interline agreement — missing a connection due to a delay on the airline's side triggers a fairly well-established process:

**You'll typically be automatically rebooked.** Many airlines now do this proactively, sometimes before you've even landed, sending a notification with your new flight details. If not, head to the airline's transfer desk or customer service counter as soon as you land.

**Your checked baggage is usually already checked through to your final destination**, so you generally don't need to worry about retrieving and rechecking it — though it's worth confirming with staff, particularly if your new routing involves a different airport or terminal.

**You may be entitled to meals, and in some cases hotel accommodation**, if the delay means an overnight wait for the next available flight. This isn't universal and depends on the airline's policy and the specific circumstances (a delay caused by weather is typically treated differently to one caused by an airline's own operational issue).

**You may be entitled to compensation** under regulations like UK261 or EU261, depending on the circumstances, the length of delay, and whether the disruption was within the airline's control. This applies to the journey as a whole when booked as a single itinerary, not just the individual missed leg.

---

## What Happens With a Self-Transfer (Separate Tickets)

If your two flights were booked independently — common when mixing a long-haul carrier with a budget regional airline, since many budget carriers don't participate in interline agreements at all — the situation is considerably less forgiving:

**The airline operating your missed flight owes you nothing.** From their perspective, you simply didn't show up for a flight you'd booked. Your ticket for that flight is forfeited.

**You will typically need to purchase an entirely new ticket** for your onward journey, at whatever fare is currently available — which, if you're booking last-minute at the airport, is often considerably more expensive than what you originally paid.

**Your checked baggage will need to be collected and rechecked**, assuming you were even able to check it through in the first place (self-transfer itineraries often require you to collect bags yourself between flights regardless).

**Compensation schemes generally don't apply across the two separate bookings.** If your first flight (on Airline A) was delayed and caused you to miss your second flight (Airline B), you may have a claim against Airline A for the original delay, but this doesn't obligate Airline B to accommodate you on the connection you missed as a result.

## Immediate Steps to Take If You've Missed a Connection

**1. Don't leave the airport, or at minimum, don't leave the transfer or arrivals area, until you understand your options.** Once you've physically left, particularly through immigration and out of the terminal, resolving things becomes considerably harder.

**2. Find the airline's transfer desk or customer service counter.** For protected connections, this is usually the fastest way to get rebooked, often faster than calling.

**3. Check the airline's app before or alongside queuing.** Many airlines now allow self-service rebooking through their app, which can be faster than waiting in a physical queue, particularly during a mass disruption event affecting many passengers at once.

**4. If it's a self-transfer situation, check availability on the same airline before booking elsewhere.** Sometimes the airline you were already booked with can offer a reasonable fare for a later flight, even without any formal obligation to do so.

**5. Keep every piece of documentation.** Boarding passes, delay notifications, receipts for any meals or accommodation you pay for yourself — these matter significantly if you later pursue a compensation claim or an insurance claim.

**6. Contact your travel insurance provider if the delay is significant**, particularly for self-transfer situations where you're bearing the cost of a new ticket yourself. Many policies cover this specific scenario, but the details and claim process vary considerably between providers.

## Does It Matter Why You Missed the Connection?

Yes, significantly — both for what happens next and for any compensation you might be entitled to.

**If the airline's own delay caused you to miss the connection** (a late departure, a mechanical issue, a late-arriving aircraft), and your flights were on a protected itinerary, the airline bears clear responsibility for rebooking you.

**If you personally arrived late to the gate** (got lost in the airport, misjudged the time needed, queued too long at a shop), even on a protected itinerary, airlines have more discretion — though in practice, most will still try to get you on the next available flight, sometimes for a rebooking fee depending on your fare type.

**If the delay was due to something outside the airline's control** (severe weather, air traffic control restrictions, security incidents), compensation entitlements are typically more limited even on protected itineraries, though rebooking assistance usually still applies.

## How to Reduce the Risk Before You Travel

This scenario is far easier to prevent than to fix after the fact. A few practical steps:

**Understand which type of booking you have before you travel** — protected or self-transfer — so you know what to expect if things go wrong, rather than discovering it under pressure at the airport.

**Build in more buffer time than the airport's stated minimum**, particularly for self-transfer connections, international connections requiring immigration, or any connection at a large, complex hub airport.

**Purchase travel insurance that explicitly covers missed connections**, and check the specific wording — not all policies cover self-arranged (self-transfer) connections in the same way they cover a single booked itinerary.

**Keep essentials in your carry-on**, so that even if your checked bag doesn't make an unexpected new routing, you're not without basics like medication or a change of clothes.

**Monitor your inbound flight's status as your travel day approaches.** If you can see early signs of delay, you can sometimes start looking at rebooking options or contacting your onward airline before you've even landed, saving valuable time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Will the airline automatically know I missed my connection?**
Usually yes, if your flights are on a single itinerary — the system typically flags this once your inbound flight lands after your connection's scheduled departure. For self-transfer bookings on entirely separate tickets, no automatic link exists, so nothing happens on your behalf; you need to take action yourself.

**Q: Can I get a refund instead of a rebooking?**
On a protected itinerary, most airlines offer rebooking as the default, though a refund for the unused portion may be available on request, particularly for more flexible fare types. On a self-transfer booking, the missed flight's ticket is typically non-refundable unless your specific fare conditions say otherwise.

**Q: Does travel insurance always cover this?**
Not always, and not always to the same extent for self-transfer versus protected connections. Check your specific policy's wording around "missed connections" and "self-arranged transfers" before assuming you're covered.

**Q: What if the missed connection ruins a non-refundable hotel or activity booking further down my trip?**
This is generally not the airline's responsibility, even on a protected itinerary, since their obligation is to get you to your ticketed destination, not to compensate for downstream disruption to your broader plans. Comprehensive travel insurance is the more relevant safety net for this specific risk.

## Final Thoughts

Missing a connecting flight ranges from a minor inconvenience to a genuinely costly problem, and the deciding factor is almost always how the flights were booked in the first place. Understanding whether you have a protected or self-transfer itinerary before you travel — not after something goes wrong — is the single most useful thing you can do to know what to expect and how to react quickly if it happens.
