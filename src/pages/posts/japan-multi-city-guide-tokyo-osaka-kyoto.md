# Japan Multi-City Guide: Tokyo, Osaka, Kyoto

Japan is one of the world's most rewarding multi-city destinations — and also one of the most forgiving to plan. The country has an extraordinarily efficient rail network, clearly defined regional highlights, and a logical geography that makes building a multi-stop itinerary genuinely straightforward.

Tokyo, Osaka, and Kyoto form the classic Japan itinerary for good reason: together they cover the country's modern energy, its culinary capital, and its historic heart. This guide walks through how to combine them, when to fly versus take the train, how long to spend in each, and the practical details that make the difference between a smooth trip and a stressful one.

---

## Why Tokyo, Osaka, and Kyoto Work So Well Together

Each city offers something genuinely distinct:

**Tokyo** is Japan's ultramodern capital — neon, technology, world-class dining, and an almost overwhelming scale. It's also most travellers' international entry point, with two major airports (Narita and Haneda).

**Kyoto** is the cultural and historic heart of Japan — over 1,600 Buddhist temples, 400 Shinto shrines, traditional wooden machiya houses, and the geisha district of Gion. It was the imperial capital for over a thousand years and remains the place to experience traditional Japan.

**Osaka** is Japan's food capital and a livelier, more informal counterpart to Kyoto's restraint. Famous for street food, particularly in the Dotonbori district, and a noticeably warmer, more talkative local culture than Tokyo.

Geographically, the three sit in a straight line down Japan's main island, Honshu — Tokyo in the east, Kyoto and Osaka close together in the west, connected by the Tōkaidō Shinkansen (bullet train) line. This makes routing simple: you move in one direction with no backtracking.

---

## The Classic Route

**London → Tokyo → Kyoto → Osaka → London**

This is the most natural order. You fly into Tokyo, travel west by train to Kyoto, continue a short distance further to Osaka, and fly home from Osaka's Kansai International Airport — or backtrack slightly to Tokyo if your return flight requires it.

**Suggested timing (10–14 days):**
- Tokyo: 4–5 days
- Kyoto: 3–4 days
- Osaka: 2–3 days

If you have additional time, Kyoto and Osaka both make excellent bases for day trips (Nara, Himeji, Kobe), which can extend the trip to two and a half or three weeks without adding more cities to the core itinerary.

---

## Flying In: Tokyo's Two Airports

Most international flights from London arrive at one of Tokyo's two airports.

**Narita (NRT)** — Tokyo's main international gateway, located about 60km east of the city centre. The Narita Express (N'EX) train takes around 60 minutes to Tokyo Station; the Keisei Skyliner is a faster, cheaper alternative to Ueno.

**Haneda (HND)** — Closer to the city centre (about 30 minutes by monorail or train) and increasingly used by international carriers, including British Airways, Japan Airlines (JAL) and ANA on the London route. If you have a choice, Haneda is generally more convenient.

**Recommendation:** Check which airport your specific flight uses — BA,JAL and ANA both fly London–Haneda direct, which saves significant transfer time compared to Narita.

## Getting Between Cities: Shinkansen vs Domestic Flights

This is the single most important practical decision in a Japan multi-city trip, and the answer is almost always the same.

### Take the Shinkansen (Bullet Train)

For the Tokyo–Kyoto–Osaka route, the Tōkaidō Shinkansen is faster, more convenient, and more comfortable than flying once you account for airport transfers.

| Route | Shinkansen time | Flight time (incl. transfers) |
|---|---|---|
| Tokyo → Kyoto | 2h15 (Nozomi) | 3–4h door to door |
| Kyoto → Osaka | 15 minutes | Not applicable — too short to fly |
| Osaka → Tokyo (return) | 2h30 (Nozomi) | 3–4h door to door |

The Shinkansen departs from city-centre stations (Tokyo Station, Kyoto Station, Shin-Osaka Station), runs every 10–15 minutes during the day, and requires no security screening beyond walking through the ticket gate. There is no comparison in convenience to flying for these distances.

### Should You Buy a Japan Rail Pass?

The Japan Rail Pass (JR Pass) offers unlimited travel on JR trains, including most Shinkansen services, for a fixed number of consecutive days. Whether it's worth it depends on your itinerary:

**Worth it if:** You're covering significant ground — for example, adding Hiroshima or Tokyo–Kyoto–Osaka return trips within the pass window.

**Not worth it if:** You're doing a simple one-way Tokyo → Kyoto → Osaka trip with no return leg. A single Tokyo–Kyoto Nozomi ticket often costs less than the pro-rated daily cost of a pass for this specific itinerary, especially since the fastest Nozomi trains are not covered by the standard JR Pass (only the slightly slower Hikari and Kodama services are included).

**How to buy:** The JR Pass must be purchased before arriving in Japan (or via the official online exchange system) and activated at a JR office on arrival. Calculate the cost of your planned individual tickets first and compare directly against the pass price for your specific dates.

## Tokyo: What to See in 4–5 Days

Tokyo is enormous, so focus on a few distinct areas rather than trying to cover everything.

**Shibuya and Shinjuku** — the iconic crossing, Shinjuku's neon-lit streets, and some of the city's best nightlife and dining.

**Asakusa** — Tokyo's old town, home to Senso-ji Temple and a more traditional, slower-paced atmosphere.

**Akihabara** — for anime, electronics, and gaming culture.

**Tsukiji Outer Market** — the original fish market area (the wholesale market moved to Toyosu in 2018, but the outer market remains and is excellent for breakfast and street food).

**A day trip to Nikko or Kamakura** — both are under 90 minutes from central Tokyo and offer a complete change of pace, with temples, shrines, and in Kamakura's case, the Great Buddha statue.

---

## Kyoto: What to See in 3–4 Days

Kyoto rewards a slower pace. Rather than rushing between famous sites, base yourself in one or two neighbourhoods and explore on foot.

**Fushimi Inari Shrine** — the thousands of vermillion torii gates climbing the mountainside. Arrive early (before 8am) to avoid the worst of the crowds.

**Arashiyama** — the bamboo grove, the Tenryu-ji temple, and the Togetsukyo Bridge. Best visited early morning for the same reason.

**Gion** — the historic geisha district, particularly atmospheric in the early evening.

**Kinkaku-ji (Golden Pavilion)** — Kyoto's most photographed sight, best visited at opening time.

**A day trip to Nara** — 45 minutes by train, home to the free-roaming deer of Nara Park and the enormous Todai-ji temple housing a 15-metre bronze Buddha.

---

## Osaka: What to See in 2–3 Days

Osaka is best approached as a food and atmosphere destination rather than a sightseeing checklist.

**Dotonbori** — the canal-side entertainment district, famous for its illuminated signage (including the giant Glico Running Man) and street food: takoyaki, okonomiyaki, and kushikatsu.

**Osaka Castle** — impressive from the outside; the interior museum is optional but the surrounding park is pleasant.

**Kuromon Ichiba Market** — Osaka's "kitchen," excellent for trying fresh seafood, wagyu skewers, and seasonal produce.

**A day trip to Himeji Castle** — about an hour from Osaka by train, and widely considered Japan's best-preserved original castle (most others were rebuilt after WWII).

## Practical Tips for a Japan Multi-City Trip

**Visa:** UK passport holders do not need a visa for tourist stays up to 90 days. Check current requirements before booking, as rules can change.

**Currency:** Japan remains a relatively cash-heavy society outside major cities, though contactless payment has expanded significantly. Carry some cash, particularly for smaller restaurants, temples, and rural areas.

**IC Cards:** Get a Suica or ICOCA card (rechargeable transit cards usable on trains, buses, and at convenience stores) immediately on arrival. These can typically be set up directly on a smartphone for UK travellers, avoiding the need for a physical card.

**Luggage forwarding:** If you don't want to carry large suitcases on the Shinkansen, Japan's takkyubin (luggage forwarding) services can send your bags ahead to your next hotel for a modest fee, typically arriving the next day.

**Booking accommodation:** Kyoto in particular books up quickly during cherry blossom season (late March–early April) and autumn foliage season (mid-November). Book 3–4 months ahead for travel during these periods.

**Best time to visit:** Spring (late March–April) for cherry blossoms and autumn (October–November) for foliage are the most popular and most beautiful times to visit, but also the most crowded and expensive. Late autumn (early November) and early spring (late February–March, before peak bloom) offer a good balance.

## Extending the Trip: Where to Add a Fourth City

If you have more than two weeks, a few natural extensions fit well into the existing route without adding significant complexity:

**Hiroshima** — 1h20 from Osaka/Kyoto by Shinkansen. The Peace Memorial Park and Museum, plus a half-day trip to Miyajima Island and its floating torii gate, justify 2 days.

**Nagoya** — sits directly between Tokyo and Kyoto on the Shinkansen line, making it an easy stopover if you want to break up the journey, though it's a lower priority than the cities above for most first-time visitors.

**Hakone** — a mountain resort town with onsen (hot springs) and views of Mount Fuji, reachable in about 90 minutes from Tokyo. Good as a 1–2 night addition either at the start or end of the Tokyo segment.

## Sample 14-Day Itinerary

**Days 1–5: Tokyo** (including a day trip to Kamakura or Nikko)
**Day 6: Travel to Kyoto** (2h15 Shinkansen)
**Days 6–9: Kyoto** (including a day trip to Nara)
**Day 10: Travel to Osaka** (15-minute Shinkansen)
**Days 10–12: Osaka** (including a day trip to Himeji)
**Day 13: Return to Tokyo or fly home from Osaka Kansai (KIX)**
**Day 14: Depart**

## Final Thoughts

Japan is one of the easiest countries in the world to build a multi-city itinerary around, thanks to its rail network and clear regional identities. Tokyo, Kyoto, and Osaka together cover an extraordinary range of experience — from futuristic cityscapes to centuries-old temples to some of the best food on the planet — without requiring complicated routing or domestic flights.

The trip essentially plans itself once you've decided how long to spend in each city. From there, it's simply a matter of booking your long-haul flights into Tokyo and out of Osaka (or back through Tokyo), reserving Shinkansen tickets, and letting the rest of the details fall into place.
