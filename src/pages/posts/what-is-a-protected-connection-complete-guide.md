# What Is a Protected Connection? A Complete Guide

**A protected connection is when two or more flights are booked together as a single itinerary, meaning the airline (or airlines involved) is responsible for getting you to your final destination — including rebooking you at no extra cost if a delay on their end causes you to miss the connection.**

This is one of the most important, and most overlooked, concepts in flight booking, particularly for multi-city trips involving several connections. This guide explains exactly what makes a connection "protected," how the underlying agreements between airlines work, and how to check whether your own booking has this protection.

---

## What Makes a Connection "Protected"?

The key factor is whether your flights share a single booking reference, issued as one ticket, rather than being two (or more) entirely separate purchases. When this is the case, a few specific things follow:

**The airline takes responsibility for the whole journey**, not just the individual flight you're sitting on. If their own delay causes you to miss a connection, they're obligated to get you to your final destination, typically via the next available flight.

**Your checked baggage is usually checked through automatically** to your final destination, rather than needing to be collected and rechecked at the connecting airport.

**You're generally covered by delay and disruption compensation schemes** (such as UK261 or EU261, where applicable) as a single journey, rather than each flight being assessed independently.

**Boarding passes and check-in are often coordinated across the whole itinerary**, sometimes allowing you to check in for your entire journey at your very first departure point.

---

## How Do Airlines Actually Protect a Connection?

There are a few different mechanisms behind this, depending on which airlines are involved:

### Same Airline

The simplest case — both flights are operated by the same carrier, booked together. The airline's own systems handle rebooking, baggage, and everything else internally, since it's all one company's operation from start to finish.

### Codeshare Agreements

Two airlines can operate a codeshare, where one airline sells seats on a flight actually operated by a different airline, under its own flight number. Codeshare flights booked as part of a single itinerary typically carry the same protections as a same-airline connection, since the whole journey is sold and ticketed as one product.

### Interline Agreements

This is the broader mechanism that allows two entirely separate airlines — not codeshare partners, just two airlines that have agreed to work together — to sell a single ticket covering both of their flights. An interline agreement means both airlines have agreed in advance to honour each other's tickets, handle baggage transfers between them, and cooperate on rebooking if something goes wrong. Many alliance partnerships (Star Alliance, Oneworld, SkyTeam) rely heavily on interline agreements between member airlines to make protected multi-airline itineraries possible.

### Alliance Membership

Airlines within the same alliance generally have interline agreements with each other as a standard part of their membership, which is part of why booking within a single alliance — even across different member airlines — is usually a low-risk way to build a protected multi-leg itinerary.

## What a Protected Connection Looks Like at Check-In and Beyond

**A single booking reference** covering your whole journey is usually the first sign — check your confirmation email or booking summary for this.

**One boarding pass process**, sometimes even allowing you to receive boarding passes for every leg of your journey at your original check-in, rather than needing to check in separately at each connecting airport.

**Baggage tags printed through to your final destination**, rather than only to your first connecting airport — worth checking at bag drop if you're ever unsure.

**A single fare rules and conditions document** governing the whole itinerary, rather than separate terms for each flight.

---

## What Happens If a Protected Connection Goes Wrong

**Automatic or assisted rebooking.** Many airlines now proactively rebook affected passengers, sometimes before you've even landed, sending updated flight details directly to your phone or email. If this doesn't happen automatically, airline staff at a transfer desk are generally able to rebook you on the spot.

**Meals and, in some cases, accommodation**, if the disruption results in an extended wait or an overnight delay — policies vary by airline and by the specific circumstances of the delay.

**Compensation eligibility**, depending on the applicable regulations, the length of the delay, and whether the cause was within the airline's control. This is assessed for your journey as a whole, not just the specific flight you missed.

It's worth being clear that "protected" doesn't mean "guaranteed problem-free" — delays and disruptions still happen. What it means is that when something does go wrong, resolving it is the airline's responsibility, not something you need to solve yourself from scratch at the airport.

---

## Protected Connection vs Self-Transfer: The Core Difference

The opposite of a protected connection is a **self-transfer** — two or more entirely separate ticket purchases, often across unrelated airlines with no interline agreement between them. In this scenario, none of the protections above apply: no automatic rebooking, no through-checked baggage, and no shared responsibility if one flight's delay causes you to miss the next.

This distinction matters enormously for how much buffer time you should realistically leave between connections, and how much risk you're taking on if something goes wrong. ## How to Check Whether Your Own Booking Is Protected

**Look for a single booking reference (PNR) covering all your flights.** If you received one confirmation email with one reference number for your entire itinerary, that's a strong sign of a protected connection.

**Check whether you were asked to select seats or provide passenger details separately for each flight**, or whether it was handled once for the whole itinerary — a single, combined process usually indicates a protected booking.

**If you booked through a search engine or travel agent, ask directly** whether the itinerary is ticketed as one journey or as separate tickets — this isn't always obvious from the price or the way results are displayed, particularly when mixing airlines.

**Be especially cautious with combinations involving budget airlines.** Many low-cost carriers don't participate in interline agreements at all, which means combining one with almost any other airline — even if a search engine shows them together as a convenient-looking itinerary — is very likely a self-transfer, regardless of how the search results are presented.

---

## Why This Matters More on Multi-City Trips

The more legs your itinerary has, the more opportunities there are for at least one connection to be unprotected, particularly if you're deliberately mixing full-service and budget carriers to get the best value across different segments — a completely reasonable way to build a trip, but one that means checking each individual connection's protection status matters more than it would on a simple round trip with one stop.

Treat this as a leg-by-leg question rather than something to assume applies uniformly across your whole itinerary. It's entirely possible for the first half of a multi-city trip to be protected and the second half to involve a self-transfer, simply because of which specific airlines happen to operate each segment.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Does booking through a single website automatically mean my connection is protected?**
No — a travel agent or search engine can sell you two separate tickets in a single transaction, which still function as a self-transfer once issued, regardless of how they were purchased together. The protection comes from how the tickets themselves are issued, not from where or how you bought them.

**Q: Are all alliance partner connections automatically protected?**
Generally yes, if booked as a single ticket, since alliance membership typically includes interline agreements between members. However, always confirm this is how your specific booking was issued rather than assuming based on the airlines' alliance membership alone.

**Q: Can a protected connection still be tight or risky?**
Yes — protection addresses what happens if something goes wrong, not how much time you have in the first place. A protected connection with an unrealistically short layover can still result in a stressful dash through the airport, even though you're covered if you don't make it.

**Q: Does travel insurance replace the need for a protected connection?**
Not entirely — travel insurance can help with costs incurred from a missed connection, but a protected connection actively gets you rebooked and to your destination, which is generally a better outcome than needing to claim on insurance after the fact.

## Final Thoughts

A protected connection is, in essence, an airline's commitment to get you all the way to your final destination, not just onto the next flight in isolation. Understanding whether your specific itinerary has this protection — checking for a single booking reference, being cautious with budget airline combinations, and confirming directly if you're ever unsure — is one of the most valuable things you can do before travelling with any multi-leg itinerary.
