# Minimum Layover Times at the World's Busiest Airports

Few things derail a carefully planned multi-city trip faster than a missed connection. And the cause is almost always the same: a layover that looked fine on paper but turned out to be too short once you factored in the realities of a specific airport — terminal changes, immigration queues, security re-screening, or simply the size of the building.

Every airport publishes a **Minimum Connection Time (MCT)** — the shortest layover the airport and airlines consider viable for a given connection type. But MCTs are minimums, not recommendations, and they don't account for delays, queues, or bad luck. This guide breaks down what MCTs actually mean, what to expect at the world's busiest hubs, and how much buffer to add on top of the official minimum.

---

## What Is a Minimum Connection Time (MCT)?

The MCT is the shortest amount of time an airport's systems will allow between a connecting flight's scheduled arrival and the next flight's scheduled departure when an itinerary is booked. If you try to book a connection shorter than the published MCT, most booking systems simply won't allow it.

MCTs vary based on:

- **Domestic-to-domestic** connections (usually the shortest)
- **Domestic-to-international** or **international-to-domestic**
- **International-to-international** (usually the longest, due to immigration and security)
- **Terminal changes** (add significant time at large airports)
- **Whether you need to re-check bags or collect and recheck them**

Critically, the *published* MCT is the floor — the absolute legal minimum a booking system will allow. It is not a guarantee that you'll comfortably make the connection, especially at a large or busy airport, or if your inbound flight is delayed.

---

## General Guidelines by Connection Type

As a rule of thumb, independent of any specific airport:

| Connection type | Recommended minimum |
|---|---|
| Domestic to domestic, same terminal | 45–60 minutes |
| Domestic to domestic, terminal change | 75–90 minutes |
| Domestic to international | 90 minutes |
| International to international, same alliance | 90–120 minutes |
| International to international, different alliance/airline | 2–3 hours |
| Any connection requiring a visa or immigration stop | 3+ hours |

These are sensible planning minimums — not the absolute legal MCTs, which are sometimes shorter. The gap between the legal minimum and a *comfortable* minimum is exactly where missed connections happen.

## Minimum Layover Times at Major Hub Airports

### London Heathrow (LHR)

Heathrow's five terminals and sprawling layout make it one of the more demanding airports for tight connections, particularly if you're changing terminals.

- **Same terminal:** 75 minutes minimum, 90 recommended
- **Terminal change:** 90 minutes minimum, 2 hours recommended
- **International to international with immigration:** 2–2.5 hours recommended

Heathrow's inter-terminal transfers can involve a bus or train ride, plus security re-screening if you're not staying within a single airline's terminal. Build in extra time if your itinerary involves British Airways (T5) connecting to a non-Oneworld carrier in another terminal.

### Dubai International (DXB)

One of the world's busiest hubs for long-haul connections, particularly via Emirates.

- **Terminal 3 to Terminal 3 (most Emirates connections):** 60–75 minutes minimum, 90 recommended
- **Terminal change required:** 2.5–3 hours recommended

DXB's Terminal 3 is enormous, and walking distances between gates can be considerable. Emirates' hub operations are generally efficient, but immigration queues for connecting passengers transiting without a UAE visa can add unpredictable time.

### Frankfurt Airport (FRA)

A major European hub, particularly for Lufthansa and Star Alliance connections.

- **Same terminal:** 45–60 minutes minimum, 75 recommended
- **Terminal 1 to Terminal 2:** 90 minutes minimum, 2 hours recommended

Frankfurt's Terminal 1 is large and can involve long walks between concourses (A, B, C, Z). Schengen-to-non-Schengen connections require an immigration check, which adds time even within the same terminal.

### Singapore Changi (SIN)

Consistently rated one of the world's best and most efficient airports, with relatively generous and well-organised connection processes.

- **Most connections, same terminal:** 60–75 minutes
- **Terminal change:** 75–90 minutes

Changi's efficiency means the official MCTs are realistic even close to the minimum, though arriving with extra time is always sensible if your budget allows exploring the airport's well-known amenities (the Jewel complex, butterfly garden, and so on).

### Hong Kong International (HKG)

A single-terminal design (with satellite concourses) makes Hong Kong relatively straightforward for connections.

- **Most connections:** 60–75 minutes minimum, 90 recommended
- **International to international with immigration:** 2 hours recommended

The satellite concourse requires an internal train, which adds a small amount of time but is well-signed and efficient.

### Chicago O'Hare (ORD) and Major US Hubs

US domestic connections are generally efficient within the same terminal, but international-to-domestic connections require full immigration and customs clearance, which significantly extends the realistic minimum.

- **Domestic to domestic, same terminal:** 45 minutes minimum, 60 recommended
- **Domestic to domestic, terminal change:** 75 minutes minimum, 90 recommended
- **International arrival connecting to domestic departure:** 2.5–3 hours recommended (you must clear immigration, collect and recheck bags, then go through TSA security again)

This last point catches many international travellers off guard. In the US, you clear immigration and customs at your *first* point of entry, even if it's not your final destination — and you must then recheck your luggage and pass through security again before any connecting flight, domestic or otherwise.

### Atlanta Hartsfield-Jackson (ATL) and Denver (DEN)

Both are vast, primarily domestic US hubs with efficient but spread-out terminal layouts.

- **Same concourse:** 45 minutes minimum
- **Different concourse (requiring the train system):** 75–90 minutes recommended

ATL in particular has a famously long terminal with an internal train (the Plane Train) connecting concourses — budget extra time if your gates are at opposite ends.

### Amsterdam Schiphol (AMS)

A relatively compact, single-terminal-building airport that's generally efficient for connections.

- **Most connections:** 50–60 minutes minimum, 75 recommended
- **Schengen to non-Schengen (or vice versa):** add 30–45 minutes for the immigration check

## Why the "Official" Minimum Isn't Always Enough

A few realities worth keeping in mind, regardless of which airport you're connecting through:

**Inbound delays are common.** A 90-minute layover assumes your first flight lands on time. Long-haul flights, in particular, regularly run 15–30 minutes behind schedule. If your layover is already close to the minimum, even a modest delay can eliminate your buffer entirely.

**Gate assignments can be unfavourable.** You have no control over which gates your flights use. A connection that looks comfortable on the map can become tight if your arrival gate and departure gate are at opposite ends of a large terminal.

**Immigration queues are unpredictable.** Particularly at major US, UK, and Schengen entry points, immigration processing times vary enormously depending on time of day, staffing levels, and how many flights have landed simultaneously.

**Security re-screening adds buffer-eating time.** If your connection requires passing through security again (common when changing terminals or in many US connections), allow at least 20–30 minutes purely for this step, on top of walking time.

## A Few Practical Rules of Thumb

**Add 30 minutes to the official MCT as a personal minimum**, especially for international connections.

**Add another 30–60 minutes if a terminal change is involved**, particularly at large airports like Heathrow, Frankfurt, or Dubai.

**Allow 2.5–3 hours for any US connection that starts with an international arrival**, due to the immigration-then-recheck-then-security sequence.

**Check whether your connecting flights are on the same ticket.** If they are, the airline is responsible for rebooking you if you miss the connection due to their delay. If they're separate (self-transfer) bookings, you bear the full risk yourself — plan accordingly with more generous buffers.

**When in doubt, choose the longer layover.** An extra hour in an airport lounge or terminal is a minor inconvenience. A missed flight, on the other hand, can derail an entire multi-city itinerary and cost significantly more to fix than the time saved by booking a tighter connection.

## Final Thoughts

Layover planning is one of the most overlooked parts of multi-city travel, and one of the easiest to get wrong. The published minimum connection time at any airport is exactly that — a minimum, not a safe target. Understanding the specific quirks of major hub airports, building in realistic buffers, and checking your connections carefully before booking will save you far more stress than the time you might save by booking the tightest available layover.
