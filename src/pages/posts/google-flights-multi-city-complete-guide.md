# Google Flights Multi City: The Complete Guide

Searching "Google Flights multi city" usually means one of two things: you can't immediately find the multi-city option and want to know where it is, or you're trying to work out whether Google Flights' version of multi-city search is actually good enough for the trip you're planning. This guide covers both — exactly where to find it, how to use it properly, and an honest assessment of what it does well versus where it starts to struggle on more complex itineraries.

---

## Where to Find Multi-City Search on Google Flights

On the Google Flights homepage, look just above the main search box. You'll see three small tabs: **Round trip**, **One way**, and **Multi-city**. It defaults to Round trip, which is exactly why the multi-city option is easy to miss on a first visit — click **Multi-city** to switch modes.

Once selected, the search box changes shape entirely: instead of a single origin, destination, and date pair, you get a stacked list of flight segments, each with its own origin, destination, and date, plus an **"Add flight"** link beneath them to include further legs.

---

## How to Set Up a Multi-City Search Properly

**Add one segment per leg of your journey.** For a trip like London → Rome → Athens → London, that's three separate segments: London–Rome, Rome–Athens, and Athens–London, each with its own date.

**Set passengers, cabin class, and baggage preferences once**, near the top of the search — these apply across the whole itinerary by default in Google Flights, so it's worth checking they suit every leg of your trip, not just the first one.

**Search, then use the sort options** (Best, cheapest, or by departure/duration) across the top of your results to compare different combinations.

---

## What Google Flights' Multi-City Search Actually Shows You

**A combined itinerary price**, reflecting the total across all your segments as searched — useful as a baseline figure, though not necessarily the absolute cheapest way to piece together the same route.

**A price graph for each individual segment**, shown beneath the results for that leg, letting you see how the fare shifts across nearby dates — genuinely one of Google Flights' stronger features, and worth checking per segment rather than only for your first or main leg.

**Layover details within a single segment**, if that particular leg itself involves a connection — for example, if your London–Athens segment routes through Frankfurt, that connection is shown and detailed as part of that segment's results.

---

## Where Google Flights' Multi-City Search Has Limits

**It treats each segment as its own independent search, not a connected journey.** This matters because the "gap" between your segments — say, the days you're in Rome before flying on to Athens — isn't evaluated by Google Flights at all in terms of feasibility. It's simply the time between your chosen dates for each segment; there's no assessment of whether that gap makes sense for your plans.

**It doesn't flag connection risk within your overall route.** If you're piecing together several segments that happen to involve tight timing between one segment's arrival and the assumptions built into your next segment's departure, Google Flights won't warn you — this is worth checking manually, particularly if any of your segments are self-transfer bookings on unrelated airlines.

**It's not built to compare a bundled multi-city fare against separately booked one-way alternatives.** Google Flights shows you its multi-city search result; comparing that total against manually priced individual one-way tickets for each leg is something you'd need to do as a separate step, since Google Flights won't do this comparison for you automatically.

**Very complex itineraries (six or more legs) can become slower and harder to visually compare**, since the interface, while functional, wasn't primarily designed around highly complex, many-legged routes.

---

## Is Google Flights Good Enough for Multi-City Planning?

For straightforward multi-city trips — two or three destinations, reasonably generous time between legs, flights on well-connected major carriers — Google Flights' multi-city search is genuinely capable and free, and there's little reason to look elsewhere.

Where it starts to show its limits is on more complex trips: longer itineraries with several legs, routes deliberately mixing budget and full-service carriers, or situations where you specifically need to understand whether your connection times are realistic for the airports involved. In these cases, Google Flights gives you the raw search results, but the evaluation of whether a specific combination of legs actually works well together is left entirely up to you.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Why can't I find the multi-city option on Google Flights?**
It's a small tab above the main search box, easy to miss since Round trip is the default. Look for the three tabs (Round trip / One way / Multi-city) just above where you enter your departure city.

**Q: Can I add more than three or four cities in Google Flights multi-city search?**
Yes, there's no fixed limit — click "Add flight" to include as many segments as your itinerary needs, though the interface becomes harder to scan visually with a large number of legs.

**Q: Does Google Flights multi-city search include budget airlines?**
Generally yes, Google Flights indexes a broad range of carriers including many budget airlines, though coverage and accuracy can vary somewhat by specific route and region.

**Q: Can I book directly through Google Flights after a multi-city search?**
No — Google Flights is a search and price comparison tool. It links through to the airline's own website or a booking partner to actually complete your purchase.

**Q: Is there a way to see whether my multi-city connections are realistic directly in Google Flights?**
Not specifically for the gaps between your separately searched segments — Google Flights shows connection details within a single segment if that leg itself involves a stopover, but doesn't evaluate the feasibility of the time between your different multi-city segments as a connected journey.

## Final Thoughts

Google Flights' multi-city search is a genuinely solid, free starting point for building almost any multi-destination itinerary, particularly for checking price trends on individual legs. Its main gap is that it searches each segment independently without evaluating how well those segments actually connect as a single trip — which is worth checking separately, especially for anything more complex than a simple two or three-city route.
