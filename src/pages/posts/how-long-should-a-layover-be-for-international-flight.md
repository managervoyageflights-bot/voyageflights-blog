# How Long Should a Layover Be for an International Flight?

**The short answer: 90 minutes to 2 hours for most international connections, and 2.5–3 hours if you're changing terminals, changing airlines, or connecting through a large hub airport.**

That's the number most travellers are looking for. But the right layover length for your specific trip depends on a handful of factors that can push it meaningfully higher — and understanding which ones apply to you is more useful than a single blanket rule.

---

## The Quick Reference

| Situation | Recommended layover |
|---|---|
| Same airline, same terminal, no immigration | 90 minutes |
| Different airline, same alliance | 2 hours |
| Different airline, no alliance relationship | 2.5–3 hours |
| Terminal change required | Add 30–60 minutes to the above |
| Connection requires clearing immigration | Add 45–90 minutes to the above |
| Large hub airport (Heathrow, Dubai, JFK) | Use the higher end of every range above |
| Small or single-terminal airport | The lower end is usually fine |

If you only take one thing from this article, it's this: **2 hours is a safe, comfortable default for most international connections.** Below that, you're increasingly relying on things going right. Above 3 hours, you're well into safety margin, unless you're specifically trying to minimise total travel time.

---

## Why 90 Minutes Is the Common Floor

Ninety minutes is roughly the minimum amount of time needed to comfortably complete the basic sequence of an international connection: deplaning, walking to your new gate, potentially clearing security or immigration, and boarding again before the gate closes. Airlines' own published Minimum Connection Times (MCTs) are often close to this figure for straightforward, same-terminal international connections — but MCTs are legal minimums, not comfortable targets.

Below 90 minutes, you're depending on your first flight landing on time, your gate being reasonably close, and no unexpected queues. Any one of these going even slightly wrong can turn a 60 or 75-minute layover into a missed flight. If you're weighing up a connection close to this threshold, [Is a 1-Hour Layover Enough?](/posts/is-a-1-hour-layover-enough) walks through exactly which factors tip a tight connection from fine into risky.

## What Pushes the Number Higher

### Immigration and Passport Control

Any connection that requires you to clear immigration — even briefly, to transit — adds the most unpredictable variable to the equation. Queue times at major entry points (the US, UK, and Schengen countries in particular) can range from a few minutes to well over an hour depending on time of day and how many flights have landed simultaneously. If your connection involves immigration, treat 2.5–3 hours as the realistic minimum, not 90 minutes.

### Changing Terminals

Large airports often split operations across multiple terminals, and moving between them can require a bus, train, or a lengthy walk — sometimes exiting and re-entering security entirely. At airports like Heathrow, JFK, or Charles de Gaulle, a terminal change alone can consume 30–45 minutes before you've even reached your new gate.

### Changing Airlines or Alliances

If your two flights are on unrelated airlines with no interline or codeshare agreement, your bags may need to be collected and rechecked, and you may need to go through check-in again rather than heading straight to security. This is far more time-consuming than a same-airline or same-alliance connection, where luggage is typically checked through automatically.

### Airport Size and Layout

Some of the world's busiest airports are also simply enormous. Dubai's Terminal 3, Heathrow's five-terminal complex, and Frankfurt's sprawling concourses can all involve considerable walking distances between gates, even within the "same" terminal. A layover that would be generous at a smaller airport can feel tight at one of these hubs. For a airport-by-airport breakdown, see [Minimum Layover Times at the World's Busiest Airports](/posts/minimum-layover-times).

### Inbound Flight Reliability

Long-haul flights, in particular, run late more often than short domestic hops — congestion at busy hub airports, weather, and knock-on delays from earlier in the day all play a role. If you can check your specific flight number's historical on-time performance, a route with frequent delays deserves a longer buffer regardless of the airport itself.

## What Allows You to Go Shorter

Not every international connection needs 2+ hours. A shorter layover — closer to the 90-minute end — is more reasonable when:

- You're staying within the same terminal
- Both flights are on the same airline or close alliance partners
- No immigration check is required (both flights are "airside" transit)
- The airport is compact and well-organised (Singapore Changi and Amsterdam Schiphol are commonly cited examples)
- Your itinerary is booked as a single, protected ticket, meaning the airline is responsible for rebooking you if something goes wrong

---

## Self-Transfer Changes the Calculation Entirely

If your two flights are booked as separate tickets rather than one linked itinerary, add significantly more buffer than any of the figures above — commonly recommended as 4+ hours, sometimes more depending on the airports involved. This is because a missed connection between two unrelated bookings is entirely your problem to solve, with no automatic rebooking or compensation from either airline.

---

## A Simple Way to Decide

Ask yourself three questions about your specific connection:

**1. Is it a single ticket, or two separate bookings?**
Single ticket → the standard ranges above apply. Self-transfer → add substantially more time.

**2. Does it involve immigration or a terminal change?**
Either one → move toward the higher end of the range, or beyond it.

**3. Is the airport large and complex, or small and simple?**
Large hub → higher end. Small, efficient airport → the lower end is usually fine.

If your answers point toward "simple, single ticket, small airport," 90 minutes to 2 hours is genuinely comfortable. If your answers point the other way on two or more of these questions, push your layover toward 3 hours or more.

---

## Final Thoughts

There's no single correct number for an international layover — but as a working default, **2 hours** covers the large majority of realistic connections comfortably, while 90 minutes remains workable for simple, low-friction transfers and 3+ hours is worth building in whenever immigration, terminal changes, or self-transfer bookings are involved.

The most common mistake isn't choosing a layover that's too short in isolation — it's not adjusting that number for the specific circumstances of your particular connection.
