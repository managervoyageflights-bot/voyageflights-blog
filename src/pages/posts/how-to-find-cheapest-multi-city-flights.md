# How to Find the Cheapest Multi-City Flights

Multi-city flights have more moving parts than a simple round trip, which means there are also more places where money can be saved — or lost — depending on how you search and book. This guide is a practical, step-by-step collection of techniques for actually finding the cheapest version of your specific multi-city itinerary, rather than settling for the first price you see on each leg.

---

## Start With the Route, Not the Price

Before touching a search engine, get your route order right. This single decision affects price more than almost any technique that follows.

**Avoid backtracking.** A route that zigzags (London → Bangkok → Tokyo → Bangkok → London) will almost always cost more than one that moves in a logical direction (London → Tokyo → Bangkok → London). Draw your route on a map before searching — if it crosses itself, it's probably not the cheapest structure available.

**Consider which city should be first and last.** Sometimes reversing your route order changes the price meaningfully, particularly if one of your cities is a stronger long-haul hub than the other. It costs nothing to search both directions and compare.

**Test adding or removing an open-jaw.** If two of your destinations are reasonably close together on the ground (reachable by train or short budget flight), consider whether flying into one and out of the other, with ground travel in between, beats flying to both and back to your start each time.

## Price Each Leg Individually, Then Look at the Whole Route

Multi-city trips are rarely priced as a single bundled fare the way a simple round trip is — in practice, you're usually assembling a route leg by leg and comparing prices at each step. A tool like VoyageFlights is built around this reality: you search and price each leg of your route individually, which gives you far more visibility into where the cost is actually coming from than a single opaque "total" figure would.

This leg-by-leg approach has a real advantage for cost-cutting: you can see exactly which segment of your trip is expensive and experiment with alternatives for that leg specifically — a different date, a different airport, or a different airline — without having to re-price the entire itinerary from scratch every time.

---

## Compare Airlines and Booking Structures Leg by Leg

Once you're pricing each leg individually, a few things are worth checking for every segment:

- Whether a budget carrier serves that specific leg more cheaply than the full-service options (particularly common on short regional hops)
- Whether flying that leg on the same airline as an adjoining leg unlocks any through-checked baggage convenience, even if booked as separate tickets
- Whether that leg is a well-known deal route with historically low fares worth timing around

Because each leg is priced and booked as its own segment in this approach, it's worth being deliberate about connection times between legs — particularly if adjoining legs end up on different airlines. A cheaper combination across legs is only a genuine saving if the connections between them are realistic; see how to judge that in [How Long Should a Layover Be for an International Flight?](/posts/how-long-should-a-layover-be-for-international-flight)

## Be Flexible on Dates — Genuinely Flexible

Date flexibility is one of the most reliable ways to reduce cost on any flight search, and it compounds across a multi-city itinerary since you have multiple dates to optimise rather than just one.

**Use a flexible date search where available.** Many search tools show a calendar or price graph across a date range rather than a single fixed date — this makes it easy to spot cheaper days at a glance.

**Shift each leg independently, not just your overall trip length.** Sometimes moving your first flight by a day makes little difference, but shifting your third leg by a day saves significantly. Test each leg separately rather than assuming the whole itinerary needs to shift together.

**Avoid Friday and Sunday departures where your schedule allows.** These are consistently among the most expensive days to fly on most routes, driven by weekend leisure travel demand.

**Consider shoulder season for at least part of your route.** If your trip spans a long enough period, timing the more expensive legs (typically long-haul) for just before or after peak season can meaningfully reduce the overall cost.

---

## Search With Different Departure and Arrival Airports

For multi-city trips in particular, being flexible on which specific airport you use — not just which city — can unlock better pricing.

**In cities with multiple airports** (London, New York, Tokyo, Bangkok, and many others), search each combination separately. A leg might be considerably cheaper departing from a secondary airport, even after factoring in the cost and time of getting there.

**Consider nearby alternative cities entirely**, not just alternative airports within the same city. A short additional train or budget flight to reposition yourself to a cheaper departure hub sometimes saves more than the extra leg costs.

---

## Check Fares in Different Currencies

This is a lesser-known technique, but genuinely effective for some routes: the same flight can occasionally be priced differently depending on which currency and country-specific version of a booking site you're searching from, due to regional pricing strategies some airlines use.

If a specific leg feels expensive, try checking the fare with your search tool's currency set to a different option, particularly the currency of one of your departure or destination countries. This won't work for every airline or route, but it costs nothing to check.

---

## Mix Full-Service and Budget Carriers Strategically

There's no rule requiring your entire multi-city itinerary to be on one airline. A common and effective approach:

**Use a full-service carrier for the expensive, long-haul legs**, where reliability, baggage allowance, and rebooking protection matter more, and where budget alternatives are often limited anyway.

**Use budget carriers for short regional hops**, where the flight itself is brief, the savings are often substantial, and the lower reliability matters less over a short distance.

This hybrid approach frequently beats either an all-full-service or all-budget itinerary on total cost, while managing risk sensibly across the trip.

---

## Set Fare Alerts Rather Than Checking Manually

Prices on multi-city routes move continuously due to dynamic pricing, not on any predictable weekly schedule. Rather than manually re-searching every few days, set up fare alerts for your specific route (many search tools and dedicated fare-tracking services offer this) and let notifications tell you when a genuine price drop occurs.

This is particularly valuable for multi-city trips booked several months in advance, where prices can fluctuate meaningfully between when you start researching and when you're ready to book.

---

## Book Long-Haul Legs Early, Stay Flexible on Short Regional Legs

Long-haul flights typically have less last-minute availability and pricing volatility works against you closer to departure — book these as early as sensibly possible, generally 2–5 months ahead depending on the route.

Short regional and domestic legs within your itinerary, particularly on budget carriers, often have more last-minute deals and pricing flexibility. It's reasonable to lock in your expensive long-haul legs first and finalise cheaper regional connections a few weeks later once your overall dates are confirmed.

---

## Don't Overlook the Total Cost, Not Just the Fare

The advertised fare isn't always the full picture, particularly when comparing budget carriers against full-service options across a multi-city trip:

**Baggage fees** on budget airlines can add up significantly across multiple legs if you need checked luggage on each one.

**Seat selection fees**, if you want to guarantee sitting together or in a specific seat type, can accumulate across several flights.

**Airport transfer costs**, if you're using a cheaper but less centrally located airport for one or more legs.

Add these into your comparison before concluding that one itinerary structure is genuinely cheaper than another.

---

## A Practical Checklist

Run through this before finalising a multi-city booking:

- [ ] Route moves in a logical direction with no unnecessary backtracking
- [ ] Priced each leg individually and checked alternative dates, airports, and airlines for the most expensive segments
- [ ] Checked flexible dates across the whole trip, not just the outbound
- [ ] Considered alternative airports for at least the most expensive leg
- [ ] Weighed full-service vs budget carriers for each individual leg
- [ ] Set a fare alert if not ready to book immediately
- [ ] Factored in baggage and seat selection costs, not just the base fare
- [ ] Verified connection times are realistic for any self-transfer legs

---

## Final Thoughts

Finding genuinely cheap multi-city flights is less about a single clever trick and more about systematically checking a handful of variables that most travellers skip: route structure, date flexibility across every leg individually, airport alternatives, and a deliberate mix of carriers across the trip. None of these techniques are complicated on their own — the value comes from applying several of them together, leg by leg, rather than accepting the first price shown for each segment.
