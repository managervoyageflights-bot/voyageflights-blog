# Open-Jaw Flights Explained: How to Use Them Smartly

If you've ever planned a road trip, an overland journey, or a holiday spanning multiple regions, you've probably run into the same frustrating problem: your flight forces you to return to the exact city you started from, even though your actual journey ends somewhere completely different.

This is exactly the problem an **open-jaw flight** solves. It's one of the simplest forms of multi-city booking, but also one of the most underused — many travellers don't realise it's an option, and end up paying for unnecessary flights or wasting days backtracking.

This guide explains what open-jaw flights are, when they make sense, how to book them, and what to watch out for.

---

## What Is an Open-Jaw Flight?

An open-jaw flight is a ticket where you fly into one city and return home from a **different** city — with the gap between the two filled by your own travel (by train, car, bus, or boat), rather than by another flight.

The term comes from how the route looks when drawn on a map: instead of a closed triangle or straight line, the route resembles an open jaw, with a gap between the two endpoints of your ground travel.

**Standard round trip:**
London → Rome → London

**Open-jaw flight:**
London → Rome ... (you travel overland) ... Florence → London

You fly into Rome, travel independently to Florence by train, and fly home from Florence. No flight connects Rome and Florence directly — that gap is yours to fill however you like.

---

## Open-Jaw vs Other Multi-City Types

Open-jaw is the simplest member of the broader multi-city family. It's worth distinguishing it from similar terms:

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Open-jaw** | Fly into A, fly home from B, travel overland between them | London → Rome (train to Florence) → London |
| **Round trip** | Fly into A, fly home from A | London → Rome → London |
| **Circle trip** | Visit multiple cities and return via a different route, but all legs are flights | London → Tokyo → Singapore → London |
| **Double open-jaw** | Both the outbound and return cities differ from the "home" pattern on both ends | New York → Rome ... Florence → Boston |

For most travellers, the single open-jaw (one gap, filled by ground travel) is the most useful and most commonly available format.

## Why Use an Open-Jaw Flight?

### You Eliminate Backtracking

This is the core benefit. If your trip naturally moves in one direction — say, travelling overland from Rome to Florence to Venice — an open-jaw ticket means you don't have to return to Rome just to catch your flight home. You fly out of Venice instead, saving time, money, and an unnecessary leg of travel.

### It Often Costs About the Same as a Round Trip

This is the detail that surprises most first-time users. Many people assume flying into one city and out of another must be significantly more expensive. In practice, open-jaw fares are frequently priced similarly to a standard round trip on the same airline or alliance — sometimes only slightly more, occasionally even less.

### It Suits Overland and Multi-Region Trips Perfectly

Open-jaw tickets are the natural choice for:

- **European city-hopping by train** — fly into Lisbon, fly home from Madrid, travel overland via Porto and Salamanca
- **Road trips** — fly into Los Angeles, fly home from San Francisco, drive the Pacific Coast Highway in between
- **Island-to-mainland travel** — fly into Bali, fly home from Jakarta, take ferries and trains through Java in between
- **Cruise itineraries** — fly into the embarkation port, fly home from the disembarkation port

### It Removes Pressure to Rush Back

Without an open-jaw ticket, many travellers feel obligated to loop back to their arrival city, sometimes cutting short time elsewhere just to make the return flight work logistically. An open-jaw ticket frees your itinerary from this constraint entirely.

## How to Book an Open-Jaw Flight

### Option 1: Use a Multi-City Search Tool

Most flight search engines that support multi-city search will also support open-jaw, since technically it's just a multi-city itinerary with one flight leg and one "ground gap." On a multi-city planner like VoyageFlights, you simply set your outbound destination and your return departure city as two different airports.

### Option 2: Book Directly Through an Airline

Most major airlines' websites have a multi-city or "multi-destination" booking option that supports open-jaw routing. Search for a multi-city itinerary with only one flight segment specified, and a separate, unconnected segment for the return.

### Option 3: Book Two One-Way Tickets

You can also simply book two separate one-way flights — one into your first city, and one home from your last city, potentially with different airlines. This gives maximum flexibility but loses the pricing advantage that a combined open-jaw fare sometimes offers, and means the two flights are not linked for rebooking purposes if either is disrupted.

## Practical Examples

### Example 1: A European Rail Trip

**London → Barcelona ... (rail/road through Valencia, Granada, Seville) ... Lisbon → London**

You fly into Barcelona, spend two weeks travelling overland through Spain and into Portugal, and fly home from Lisbon. No backtracking to Barcelona required.

### Example 2: A US West Coast Road Trip

**London → Seattle ... (drive down the Pacific Coast Highway through Portland, San Francisco, Los Angeles) ... San Diego → London**

A classic open-jaw use case. Flying into Seattle and out of San Diego eliminates the need to drive back north or book a return flight from Seattle.

### Example 3: A Southeast Asia Overland Trip

**London → Bangkok ... (overland through Cambodia and into Vietnam) ... Ho Chi Minh City → London**

Fly into Bangkok, travel overland by bus and train through Cambodia into southern Vietnam, and fly home from Ho Chi Minh City.

## What to Watch Out For

### Visa and Entry Requirements at Both Ends

Since you're effectively crossing into and out of countries at two different points, double-check entry requirements for the actual cities you're flying into and out of — not just the country as a whole, if your trip spans more than one nation.

### Airport Tax and Fee Differences

Departure taxes vary by country and even by airport. An open-jaw ticket exposes you to the departure tax of whichever city you're flying home from, which may be slightly different from your arrival city's. This is rarely significant, but worth being aware of.

### Ground Transport Planning

The "gap" in your open-jaw ticket is entirely your responsibility to plan. Make sure realistic transport options exist for the route you're imagining — checking train schedules, road conditions, or ferry timetables before committing to specific flight dates.

### Returning Luggage Allowances May Differ

If your outbound and return flights are with different airlines (common with open-jaw tickets, especially if booked as two separate one-ways), baggage allowances can differ. Check both separately rather than assuming consistency.

### Travel Insurance Coverage

Make sure your travel insurance policy covers the full duration and the full geographic scope of your trip, including the overland portion between your arrival and departure cities.

## Open-Jaw and Pricing: What to Expect

As a general rule of thumb, when comparing an open-jaw fare to a standard round trip on the same route pairing:

- **Same airline/alliance, similar regions:** Often within 10–15% of round trip pricing
- **Different airlines on each leg:** Pricing varies more, but searching independently can sometimes find a cheaper combination than a bundled open-jaw fare
- **Long-haul intercontinental open-jaws:** Can occasionally be cheaper than round trip, especially on routes where one-way fares are well-priced (some European and Asian carriers price one-ways more favourably than others)

The only way to know for certain is to compare. Search the open-jaw combination on a multi-city tool, then separately search what two one-way tickets would cost, and compare both against a standard round trip to either city.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Is an open-jaw flight the same as a multi-city flight?**
Open-jaw is a specific, simpler type of multi-city flight — one with only a single flight gap filled by your own ground travel, rather than multiple flight segments connecting several cities.

**Q: Can I do an open-jaw trip within the same country?**
Yes. Domestic open-jaw tickets work the same way — for example, flying into Edinburgh and home from London after a UK road trip, or flying into Cairns and home from Sydney after exploring Australia's east coast.

**Q: Do I need a visa for both cities if they're in the same country?**
No — if both your arrival and departure cities are within the same country, you only need whatever visa or entry permission applies to that country as a whole.

**Q: What if my flights are with two completely different airlines?**
This is common and generally fine, but remember that the two tickets are not linked. If one flight is delayed or cancelled, the other airline has no obligation to accommodate you, so build in a reasonable buffer between your ground travel arrival and your departure flight.

**Q: Is open-jaw available on budget airlines?**
Most low-cost carriers don't offer a true open-jaw booking system — they sell one-way tickets, so you simply book two separate one-ways with different origin and destination cities. The effect is the same as an open-jaw, even if it's not labelled that way.

The open-jaw flight is one of the most practical tools in independent travel planning, and one of the most overlooked. Once you understand that you don't have to fly back to your starting point — that you can simply fly out from wherever your trip naturally ends — entire categories of travel become easier to plan: road trips, rail journeys, island hopping, and any itinerary that moves in one direction rather than looping back on itself.
