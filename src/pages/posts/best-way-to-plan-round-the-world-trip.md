# What Is the Best Way to Plan a Round-the-World Trip?

A round-the-world trip is the most ambitious version of multi-city travel — typically spanning several continents, months rather than weeks, and enough complexity that "winging it" tends to produce either an exhausting itinerary or a significantly more expensive one than necessary.

The best approach isn't complicated, but it does require making a handful of decisions in the right order. This guide walks through that order: routing direction, whether to use a round-the-world ticket or book individually, how to pace the trip, and the practical planning steps that make the difference between an incredible year and a stressful one.

---

## Step 1: Decide on Your Route Direction

Every round-the-world trip needs an overall direction — generally either eastward or westward — determined mainly by prevailing flight routes and where you want to spend the most time.

**Eastward routes** (e.g. starting in Europe, moving through Asia, then the Pacific, then the Americas, back to Europe) tend to align well with many round-the-world ticket alliances and generally follow a natural flow with the jet stream on longer legs.

**Westward routes** work equally well and are simply the mirror image — the choice mostly comes down to which region you want to visit first while your energy and budget are freshest, and which region you'd rather save for last.

**The golden rule remains the same as any multi-city trip: keep moving in one direction.** A round-the-world trip that criss-crosses back and forth between continents multiple times is both exhausting and considerably more expensive than one that flows in a single loop.

---

## Step 2: Decide Between an RTW Ticket and Individually Booked Flights

This is the biggest structural decision for a round-the-world trip, and it's worth understanding both options properly before choosing.

### Round-the-World (RTW) Tickets

Airline alliances (Star Alliance, Oneworld, SkyTeam) offer dedicated round-the-world tickets: a single ticket covering multiple flights across their partner airlines, typically priced by total mileage flown or number of continents visited, rather than by individual route.

**Advantages:**
- Often cheaper than booking the equivalent long-haul legs individually, particularly for itineraries covering 4+ continents
- Single itinerary, so protected in the same way any linked booking is — the alliance is responsible for getting you through your full route
- Encourages (and sometimes requires) committing to a rough plan in advance, which suits travellers who want structure

**Limitations:**
- You're restricted to the specific alliance's airlines and partner network — if a route you want isn't well served by that alliance, you may need to add a separate flight anyway
- Changes to already-booked segments can be restrictive and sometimes costly
- Doesn't naturally accommodate budget carriers, which may offer cheaper options for shorter regional hops within your route

### Individually Booked Flights

Building your own route leg by leg, mixing airlines and alliances freely, gives you complete flexibility but requires considerably more planning effort.

**Advantages:**
- No restriction to a single alliance's network — freely combine full-service long-haul carriers with budget regional airlines
- Easier to adjust individual legs as your plans evolve during the trip
- Can be cheaper for itineraries that lean heavily on budget carriers for regional legs, where an RTW ticket's alliance network doesn't offer great value

**Limitations:**
- More self-transfer risk if you're mixing unrelated airlines, since these separate bookings generally aren't linked
- Requires more research and ongoing management, particularly for connection times between unrelated carriers
- No built-in protection across your whole route the way a single RTW ticket provides

### Which Should You Choose?

As a rough guide: **RTW tickets tend to suit itineraries with 4+ long-haul continental legs and a reasonably fixed plan.** **Individually booked flights tend to suit itineraries with more regional hopping, more flexibility needs, or heavier reliance on budget carriers for shorter legs.**

Many experienced round-the-world travellers use a hybrid: an RTW ticket or a few individually booked long-haul legs for the big continental jumps, combined with separately booked budget flights for shorter regional exploration within each continent.

## Step 3: Decide How Many Countries and Regions to Cover

This is where round-the-world trips most commonly go wrong. The temptation to fit in "one more country" is constant, and the result is often a year that feels like a checklist rather than a genuinely lived experience in each place.

**A reasonable starting framework for a 10–12 month trip:**
- 4–6 major regions (e.g. Southeast Asia, Australia/New Zealand, South America, and one or two others)
- Within each region, 2–4 weeks minimum to allow for slower travel, unplanned discoveries, and simple rest

**Resist the urge to add destinations just because they're "on the way."** A country that's geographically close to your route isn't automatically worth visiting if it means compressing time elsewhere. It's genuinely better to properly experience fewer places than to rush through more.

---

## Step 4: Build in Deliberate Slow Periods

Round-the-world trips are long enough that travel fatigue becomes a real factor, usually appearing somewhere around the 3–4 month mark for most travellers.

**Plan at least one genuinely extended stay (2+ weeks in one place) roughly every 6–8 weeks.** This isn't wasted time — it's what allows the rest of the trip to remain enjoyable rather than becoming an endurance exercise.

**Consider slower travel methods for at least part of the route.** Overland travel (trains, buses, boats) between destinations within a region, rather than flying every single hop, changes the texture of a long trip considerably and is often part of what makes round-the-world travel memorable rather than just a long sequence of airports.

---

## Step 5: Sequence Your Visa Applications

Round-the-world trips often involve visas for multiple countries, some of which have long processing times or need to be applied for from a specific location (sometimes requiring you to be in a neighbouring country to apply).

**Research every visa requirement before finalising your route order**, not after. Some countries' visa rules affect how long you can stay, whether you need proof of onward travel, and whether you can apply on arrival or need advance approval — all of which can affect your pacing decisions in Step 3.

**Sequence countries with complex visa requirements earlier in your planning process**, even if you visit them later in the actual trip, so there's no risk of a delayed visa disrupting your route once you're already travelling.

---

## Step 6: Set a Realistic Total Budget

Round-the-world budgets vary enormously depending on region mix and travel style, but a structured approach helps:

**Break the budget by region, not just by category.** Southeast Asia and South America are generally far cheaper day-to-day than Australia, New Zealand, Western Europe, or Japan. Weighting your time (and budget) accordingly, rather than assuming a flat daily rate across the whole trip, produces a far more realistic total.

**Budget separately for the flights themselves versus daily living costs.** Whether via an RTW ticket or individually booked flights, get this cost locked in early, since it's the least flexible part of the budget once booked.

**Build in a genuine contingency — 15–20% of your total budget** — for unexpected costs, which are more likely on a long trip than a short one simply due to the number of moving parts involved.

---

## Step 7: Plan Your Insurance and Health Preparation Early

**Long-stay, multi-country travel insurance is a different product from standard trip insurance** — check that any policy you're considering genuinely covers a trip of this length and geographic spread, since some standard travel insurance policies cap coverage at 30, 60, or 90 days.

**Research vaccination requirements for your full route as early as possible.** Some vaccines require multiple doses spread over weeks or months, so this needs more lead time than a typical two-week holiday would.

**Consider what ongoing healthcare access looks like for the specific regions on your route**, particularly if you have any ongoing medical needs — this varies considerably between, say, Western Europe and more remote parts of Southeast Asia or South America.

---

## A Simplified Planning Order

To bring this together, a sensible sequence looks like:

1. Decide your overall route direction (eastward or westward) and rough regions
2. Choose between an RTW ticket, individually booked flights, or a hybrid
3. Research visa requirements across your full route
4. Set your region-by-region time allocation and budget
5. Book your major long-haul legs (RTW ticket or individual flights)
6. Book initial accommodation for your first stop in each major region, leaving the rest flexible
7. Arrange long-stay travel insurance and complete any required vaccinations
8. Leave the finer details — specific city-to-city routing within each region — flexible to adjust as you go

---

## Final Thoughts

The best way to plan a round-the-world trip isn't to map out every single day in advance — it's to make the structural decisions carefully (route direction, ticket type, region pacing, visas, and budget) and then leave enough flexibility within that structure to actually enjoy the trip as it unfolds. The trips that go well are rarely the most tightly scheduled ones; they're the ones where the big logistics were handled properly early on, leaving room to slow down, change plans, and follow what's actually interesting once you're there.
